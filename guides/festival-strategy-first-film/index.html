Festival strategy for your first film

Premiere status is something you can spend only once. Here is how it works, what the odds really are, and a submission plan that doesn't burn your best shot.

Updated October 20269 min read

Most first-time filmmakers treat festivals like a lottery: finish the film, upload it to every call for entries that fits, and hope. The problem is that the order you submit in matters as much as the film. A screening at a small festival, or a public link on YouTube, can quietly make you ineligible for the festivals that would have changed your career.

This guide covers the rules that trip people up most, using the official regulations of the festivals themselves. Festivals are stage 8 of the film roadmap, which lists every job from script to release.

Premiere status is a currency

Big festivals want to be first. They rank films by where they have already screened, and the labels are roughly these:

  • World premiere: the film has never been shown publicly anywhere.
  • International premiere: it has only been shown in its country of origin.
  • Regional premiere (European, North American and so on): first screening on that continent.
  • National premiere: first screening in the festival's country.

Every screening moves you down that list, and you can never move back up. Here is what two of the festivals most first-time directors dream about actually require:

Festival / sectionMinimum premiere status
Sundance, U.S. featuresWorld premiere
Sundance, international featuresInternational premiere
Sundance, shortsNone, but the short must be recently completed and not yet available to the public
Berlinale, CompetitionWorld or international premiere. German films must be world premieres
Berlinale, Panorama, Forum, Generation, PerspectivesAt least a European premiere

Berlin also states that world premieres are preferred in all sections, and that a festival screening or release in your film's main country of origin is accepted (except for German films). In other words: a premiere at home doesn't automatically close the door to Berlin, but a premiere in another country does close the Competition.

"Online" counts as a screening

This is the mistake that costs the most. If your film is publicly available on the internet, many major festivals will not take it, even if it was only up for a few weeks.

  • Berlinale: films that have been broadcast on television or presented on the internet or VOD are not eligible.
  • Sundance: U.S. features that have been made available on any streaming or online platform, free or paid, are ineligible.

Stephen Follows studied the rules of 10,549 festivals worldwide: 11.1% explicitly refuse films that are already online, 16% explicitly accept them, and 72.8% don't say. That silent majority is exactly why you should ask before you post.

Private, password-protected screener links are not a public release and are the normal way festivals watch submissions. A public YouTube or Vimeo link is a release.

The real odds

Knowing the numbers helps you plan without taking rejections personally. Sundance published its own figures for the 2026 edition:

4,255feature films submitted to Sundance 2026
90features selected, about 2% of submissions
40%of the selected feature directors were first-time filmmakers

Two things are true at once. The top festivals are very hard to get into, and they genuinely do program first films: 36 of the 90 selected features were by first-time feature directors. A good first film is not at a disadvantage. A first film that wasted its premiere is.

Below the top tier there are hundreds of festivals with very different reach. The FIAPF, the international federation of film producers associations, accredits 50 festivals in 29 countries, and its latest reform gave "A" status to 17 of them, including Cannes, Berlin, Venice, Locarno, San Sebastián, Toronto, Busan and Karlovy Vary. Accreditation is not the only measure of a good festival, but it tells you which events the industry treats as major launch pads.

A plan that protects your premiere

The goal is simple: give the festivals that matter most to your film a chance to say yes before you spend your premiere anywhere else.

1. Build a ranked list, not a long one

Pick 10 to 20 festivals that fit your film's genre, length, language and budget level, and sort them in three tiers: the dream festivals, the strong ones that buyers and press attend, and the reliable ones where you are likely to be selected.

2. Map the calendar backwards

Write down each festival's dates, final deadline and the date it usually notifies filmmakers. Submit top tier first. Only accept a lower-tier invitation once the festivals above it have answered, or once you have decided you no longer want them.

3. Budget the fees

Fees add up fast. Across 4,631 festivals, Stephen Follows found average submission fees between $30 and $55 for shorts and between $47 and $87 for features, depending on how early you submit. Sundance's 2027 feature fee ranges from $80 to $125. Submitting early is the cheapest saving available, and many festivals will grant a fee waiver if you ask politely and explain why your film fits.

4. Keep the film offline

No public trailer containing large parts of the film, no public link, no online festival without checking the rules of the festivals still on your list. A teaser is fine; the film is not.

5. Decide what success means

For some films the right first stop is a smaller festival with a strong market or industry section, where you meet sales agents and distributors. A premiere is worth the most where the people you need are in the room.

Shorts follow different rules

Short films are usually more flexible. Sundance, for example, sets no premiere requirement for shorts, only that the film is recently completed and hasn't already been made public. Many festivals that require premieres for features don't for shorts.

Shorts also have their own major stops. Two of FIAPF's 17 "A" festivals are specialised: Clermont-Ferrand for short films and Annecy for animation.

If you are aiming for the Oscars, some festivals are Academy qualifying: winning their top short film prize can make a short eligible. The list changes every year, so check the current one on the Academy's official rules before you plan around it.

Checklist before you submit anywhere

  • 01Is the film offline everywhere, with only private screener links?
  • 02Do you have a ranked list of 10 to 20 festivals in three tiers?
  • 03Have you read the premiere rules of each one, on the festival's own site?
  • 04Do your submission dates let the top tier answer first?
  • 05Have you set a fee budget and asked for waivers where it makes sense?

Festival rules change every edition. Everything above was checked against the festivals' official pages in October 2026, but always read the current regulations before you submit.
